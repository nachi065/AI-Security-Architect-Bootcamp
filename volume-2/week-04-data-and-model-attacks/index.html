    Week 4: Data and model attacks¶

    Volume 2 · Weeks 3-4: Automated red teaming, data and model attacks

    You will be able to: explain and demonstrate the main attacks on data and models, and measure how much they reveal.

    Core concepts

    • Data poisoning. Corrupting training or fine-tuning data (or retrieval corpora) to degrade or steer a model. Targeted poisoning aims at specific inputs.
    • Backdoors. A model behaves normally until a trigger appears, then produces attacker-chosen output. Hard to detect by ordinary testing because normal accuracy stays high.
    • Model extraction. Copying a model's behaviour by querying it and training a substitute. Cost depends on query access, output detail (probabilities leak more than labels) and model size.
    • Membership inference. Deciding whether a specific record was in the training data, usually by exploiting overconfidence on training examples. Privacy-relevant for regulated data.
    • Embedding inversion. Recovering approximate text from stored embeddings, so vector databases are not safely "anonymous".
    • Sensitive information disclosure and system-prompt leakage. Models may repeat training data, retrieved documents or hidden instructions. Treat any secret placed in a prompt as recoverable.

    Study plan (about 9 hours)

    1. NIST AI 100-2 sections on poisoning, extraction and privacy attacks, plus the matching OWASP LLM entries, about 3 hours.
    2. Read the original papers or survey articles for membership inference and model extraction (search for the current standard references; I do not name specific papers here), about 2 hours.
    3. Lab and deliverable, about 4 hours.

    Guided lab: measure what leaks

    1. Reuse your Week 10 scikit-learn classifier from Volume 1. Implement a simple extraction exercise: query it with generated inputs, train a substitute on the answers, and measure how closely the substitute matches the original as the number of queries grows. Plot fidelity against query budget.
    2. Run a basic membership inference test on the same model: compare confidence on training versus held-out records and compute how well a threshold separates them. Repeat with a deliberately overfitted model and with a regularised one.
    3. Against your local LLM, test system-prompt leakage with canary text in the prompt, using at least five phrasing styles. Record the success rate for each.
    4. Test sensitive disclosure through the RAG app: plant fake personal records in the corpus and measure how often they surface to questions that should not return them.
    5. For each experiment, write what an attacker would need (access, queries, knowledge), what they gain, and one defence with the metric that would show it works.

    Deliverable (commit to v2-week04/): an attack write-up with measured results, charts and a defence column.

    Self-check

    1. Why does returning probabilities make extraction easier?
    2. Why does overfitting raise membership-inference risk?
    3. Why are backdoors hard to find by normal accuracy testing?
    4. Why can embeddings not be treated as anonymised data?
    5. What should you never put in a system prompt, and why?
