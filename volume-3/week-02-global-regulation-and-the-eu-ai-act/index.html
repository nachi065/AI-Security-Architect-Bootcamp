    Week 2: Global regulation and the EU AI Act¶

    Volume 3 · Weeks 1-2: Governance operating model and global regulation

    You will be able to: classify AI use cases under the EU AI Act, identify the obligations that follow, and explain which dates currently apply.

    Status as of 5 October 2026 (verified from the sources listed at the end; re-check before relying on it)

    • The EU AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It was amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, signed on 8 July 2026, published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force from 27 July 2026.
    • The prohibited-practices rules have applied since 2 February 2025, and the obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) model providers since 2 August 2025.
    • Obligations for stand-alone high-risk systems (Annex III) were deferred from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. Obligations for AI embedded in products regulated under other EU safety law (Annex I) were deferred to 2 August 2028.
    • Per the sources I read, the Article 50 transparency duties (telling people they are dealing with AI, labelling synthetic content, disclosing deepfakes) were not deferred and apply from 2 August 2026. Some sub-obligations, such as technical marking for systems already on the market, appear to have a later grace date (2 December 2026 in one source). Law-firm summaries vary on how this grace period works, so read the Official Journal text.
    • The Omnibus also adds two new prohibited practices (certain intimate-imagery and child-abuse-material generation) and softens the AI-literacy duty in Article 4 to measures supporting literacy.

    Key EU AI Act dates as of the build date, with a marker for today. Verify against the Official Journal.

    Teaching summary of the EU AI Act structure. Check the regulation text for the exact rules.

    Core concepts

    • Risk-based structure. Prohibited practices (banned), high-risk systems (strict obligations), transparency-risk systems (disclosure duties), and minimal-risk systems (no specific obligations). GPAI models have their own obligations.
    • Roles. The Act distinguishes providers (who develop or place a system on the market) from deployers (who use it) and others such as importers and distributors. Your obligations depend on your role.
    • High-risk obligations (when they apply). Risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency to deployers, human oversight, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, conformity assessment and registration. Read these as a ready-made controls list.
    • Penalties. Per the CSA note I read, up to 35 million euros or 7% of global annual turnover for prohibited practices, and up to 15 million euros or 3% for most other violations. Confirm in the regulation text.
    • Beyond the EU. The EU Act is one model among several. Note how other jurisdictions you work in handle AI (voluntary frameworks, sector rules, privacy law) as you reach Weeks 3 and 4.

    Study plan (about 8 hours)

    1. Read the Act's structure (recitals skim, then Articles 1-6, 9-15, 26, 50-55, and Annex III) from the official text on EUR-Lex, about 4 hours. You do not need to memorise articles; learn where to look.
    2. Read the CSA research note on the Omnibus (linked in the sources section) and one law-firm summary, about 1.5 hours.
    3. Lab and deliverable, about 2.5 hours.

    Guided lab: classify ten use cases

    For each use case below, decide: prohibited, high-risk, transparency-risk, or minimal; and whether your organisation would be provider or deployer. Write two sentences of reasoning citing the relevant article or annex.

    1. A website chatbot that answers customers' shipping questions.
    2. A tool that ranks job applicants' CVs.
    3. A model that decides consumer-loan eligibility.
    4. An email spam filter.
    5. A government system that scores citizens on social behaviour and restricts their services.
    6. A marketing tool that generates product images.
    7. An AI system that grades exams used for university admission.
    8. A system that infers employees' emotions from webcam video at work.
    9. An internal code assistant used by developers.
    10. Predictive maintenance for factory machinery.

    Only after you finish, compare with this indicative key (a teaching answer, not legal advice; edge cases depend on exact facts and the current text): (1) transparency duty to tell users it is AI; (2) high-risk (employment); (3) high-risk (access to essential services); (4) minimal; (5) prohibited; (6) transparency duty for synthetic content; (7) high-risk (education); (8) very likely prohibited in the workplace, with narrow exceptions, so verify against Article 5; (9) minimal for you as a deployer, with obligations falling mainly on the model provider; (10) minimal unless it is a safety component of a regulated product, which would bring Annex I into play.

    Deliverable (commit to volume3/week2/): a classification memo of two to three pages with your reasoning for each case, a list of obligations triggered, the dates that apply, and a note of what you are unsure about.

    Self-check

    1. What are the four risk categories, and which one carries the heaviest obligations?
    2. What is the difference between a provider and a deployer?
    3. Which obligations did the Omnibus defer, and which did it leave on the original schedule?
    4. Why is it unsafe to rely on a summary of a regulation that is weeks old?
    5. How would a high-risk obligation such as logging map to a technical control you built in Volume 1?
