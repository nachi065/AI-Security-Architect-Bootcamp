    Week 5: Policy stack, control objectives and the risk register¶

    Volume 3 · Weeks 5-6: Policy, risk register and third-party risk

    You will be able to: write a coherent set of AI policies, link them to testable control objectives, and maintain a risk register that decision-makers can use.

    Policies state what must be true; standards and procedures say how; control objectives make it testable.

    Core concepts

    • The policy stack. Layers from broad to specific. A top-level AI governance policy sets principles, roles and scope. Beneath it sit focused policies: acceptable use of AI (including generative AI and shadow AI), model lifecycle (build, validate, deploy, retire), data used for AI, third-party AI, and AI incident management. Below policies sit standards and procedures that say exactly how.
    • Control objectives. A policy statement says what must be true ("AI systems that process personal data must have a documented lawful basis"). A control objective restates it as something you can test. Controls are the mechanisms that achieve it.
    • AI impact assessments. A structured review of an AI use case's effects on individuals, the organisation and society. Many privacy laws require data protection impact assessments for high-risk processing; an AI impact assessment can build on that work instead of duplicating it. Design one form that satisfies both where possible.
    • Risk register. A living list of risks with owner, likelihood, impact, existing controls, residual risk, treatment decision and review date. Write each risk as cause, event and consequence, not as a vague label such as "bias".
    • Treatment options. Mitigate, transfer, avoid or accept. Acceptance needs a named senior owner and a review date.
    • Linking technical evidence. Your Volume 2 findings are risk inputs. A prompt-injection finding with a measured success rate is stronger evidence than a general statement that injection is possible.

    Study plan (about 8 hours)

    1. Read two or three published organisational AI policies or templates from reputable bodies (search for current public examples), noting structure and length, about 2 hours.
    2. Read the risk-assessment sections of the NIST AI RMF and the ISO/IEC 23894 summary, about 2 hours.
    3. Lab and deliverable, about 4 hours.

    Guided lab: policy pack and populated risk register

    1. Draft a pack for your fictional enterprise: a top-level policy (two pages), an acceptable-use policy (two pages), a model lifecycle policy (two to three pages) and an AI impact assessment form (two pages).
    2. For each policy, extract at least five control objectives and link them to your Week 3 control set.
    3. Build a risk register with at least 15 entries covering the system you built in Volumes 1 and 2. Include risks such as prompt injection, data leakage through retrieval, over-privileged agent actions, model-supply-chain compromise, regulatory non-compliance, and vendor lock-in.
    4. For each entry, rate inherent risk, list controls, rate residual risk, and decide treatment. Where a Volume 2 finding exists, cite it.
    5. Review the register for two entries that you rated too optimistically. Explain what evidence would change the rating.

    Deliverable (commit to volume3/week5/): the policy pack and the populated risk register.

    Self-check

    1. What is the difference between a policy, a standard and a procedure?
    2. How does a control objective differ from a policy statement?
    3. Rewrite "AI bias" as a properly framed risk (cause, event, consequence).
    4. Who may accept a high residual risk, and what must accompany that decision?
    5. How can one assessment serve both a privacy impact assessment and an AI impact assessment?
