    Week 10: Machine learning foundations and the attack surface¶

    Volume 1 · Part 4 (Weeks 10-13): ML, LLMs, RAG, agents and MCP

    Part goal

    build the systems that Volumes 2-4 will attack, govern and re-architect. Keep your code clean and your repository tidy; you will return to it.

    You will be able to: train a simple classifier, explain the ML lifecycle, and demonstrate two basic attacks on a model.

    Each stage of the lifecycle has its own attack. This is the map for your Week 10 attack-surface note.

    Core concepts

    • Learning types. Supervised (labelled data), unsupervised (structure without labels). Features, labels, training versus inference, train/validation/test split.
    • Evaluation. Accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and the confusion matrix. Accuracy misleads on imbalanced data (a model that always says "benign" can look 99% accurate).
    • MLOps lifecycle. Data collection, preparation, training, evaluation, packaging, deployment, monitoring, retraining. Each stage has its own risks.
    • Adversarial ML primer. Evasion (altering inputs at inference time to cause errors), poisoning (corrupting training data), model extraction (copying a model through queries), inference attacks (learning about training data, such as membership inference). NIST publishes an adversarial machine learning taxonomy (AI 100-2; check the current edition).

    Study plan (about 9 hours)

    1. An introductory ML course or book chapters covering supervised learning and evaluation, about 4 hours.
    2. scikit-learn user guide sections on classification and metrics, about 1.5 hours.
    3. Lab and deliverable, about 3.5 hours.

    Guided lab: train, then attack

    1. In a Jupyter notebook, train a scikit-learn classifier on a small public tabular dataset (the library ships some, or use a spam or malware-feature dataset).
    2. Report accuracy, precision, recall and the confusion matrix. Explain which metric matters for your task.
    3. Evasion demo. Take correctly classified samples and make small changes to features until the prediction flips. Record how big the change had to be.
    4. Poisoning demo. Flip the labels of a fraction of the training data (for example 5%, 10%, 20%), retrain, and plot how the test metrics change.
    5. Summarise both attacks in a short table: attack, access needed, effect measured, one possible defence.

    Deliverable (commit to week10/): the notebook and a one-page ML attack-surface map covering data, training, model, serving and monitoring.

    Self-check

    1. Why can high accuracy still mean a useless model?
    2. What is the difference between evasion and poisoning?
    3. What access does an attacker need to extract a model?
    4. Where in the MLOps lifecycle would you add integrity checks?
    5. What did your label-flipping experiment show about robustness?
